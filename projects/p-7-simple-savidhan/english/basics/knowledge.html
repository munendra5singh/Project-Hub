1. Constitution at a Glance & History

What is the Constitution? The Constitution of India is the supreme law of the land. It establishes the political code, structure, procedures, powers, and duties of government institutions and guarantees fundamental rights to citizens.

Key Basic Facts
  • Supreme Law: Every legislation passed by Parliament or State Legislatures must conform to the Constitution.
  • Adopted Date: November 26, 1949 (Celebrated as Constitution Day / Samvidhan Divas).
  • Enforcement Date: January 26, 1950 (Celebrated as Republic Day).
  • Handwritten Masterpiece: Calligraphed by Prem Behari Narain Raizada in English and Hindi.
Historical Background (1773 – 1947)
Act / Reform Key Constitutional Significance
Regulating Act 1773First step by British Govt to control East India Company; created Governor-General of Bengal.
Pitt's India Act 1784Distinguished political and commercial functions; established Board of Control.
Charter Act 1833Made Governor-General of Bengal the Governor-General of India (Lord William Bentinck).
Govt of India Act 1858Transferred power from East India Company to the British Crown (Viceroy created).
Indian Councils Act 1861/1892Introduced portfolio system and expanded representative legislative council powers.
Morley-Minto Reforms (1909)Introduced communal representation (separate electorates) for Muslims.
Montagu-Chelmsford (1919)Introduced Dyarchy in provinces and bicameralism at the Centre.
Govt of India Act 1935Provided for All-India Federation, Provincial Autonomy, and 3 Legislative Lists.
Indian Independence Act 1947Ended British rule, declared India independent, and created two dominions (India & Pakistan).

2. Constituent Assembly & Drafting Committee

The Constituent Assembly was a sovereign body formed under the Cabinet Mission Plan of 1946 to frame the Constitution of India.

First MeetingDec 9, 1946
Interim PresidentDr. Sachchidananda Sinha
Permanent PresidentDr. Rajendra Prasad
Constitutional AdviserSir B. N. Rau
Drafting ChairmanDr. B. R. Ambedkar
The Drafting Committee & Dr. B. R. Ambedkar

Set up on August 29, 1947, the Drafting Committee was tasked with preparing the draft Constitution. Dr. B. R. Ambedkar, known as the "Father of the Indian Constitution," guided the discussions and defended provisions in assembly debates.

  • Members: Dr. B. R. Ambedkar (Chairman), N. Gopalaswamy Ayyangar, Alladi Krishnaswamy Ayyar, Dr. K. M. Munshi, Syed Mohammad Saadullah, N. Madhava Rau, T. T. Krishnamachari.
  • Objectives Resolution: Moved by Jawaharlal Nehru on Dec 13, 1946; laid down the fundamentals and philosophy of the constitutional structure.

4. Salient Features & The Preamble

Salient Features: Longest written constitution, mixture of rigidity and flexibility, federal system with unitary bias, parliamentary form of government, integrated and independent judiciary, universal adult franchise, and secular state.

Key Words in the Preamble Explained
  • Sovereign: Independent authority without external interference.
  • Socialist: Added by 42nd Amendment (1976); aims to eliminate inequalities in income and status.
  • Secular: Added by 42nd Amendment (1976); equal respect and support for all religions.
  • Democratic: Government derived from the sovereign will of the people.
  • Republic: Elected Head of State (President) rather than a hereditary monarch.
  • Justice: Social, Economic, and Political justice guaranteed.
  • Liberty: Liberty of thought, expression, belief, faith, and worship.
  • Equality: Equality of status and opportunity.
  • Fraternity: Assuring the dignity of the individual and the unity & integrity of the Nation.

5. Fundamental Rights (Articles 12–35)

Guaranteed in Part III, Fundamental Rights are justiciable civil liberties protected directly by the judiciary. They act as a check on executive and legislative power.

Right to EqualityArt. 14 – 18
Right to FreedomArt. 19 – 22
Against ExploitationArt. 23 – 24
Freedom of ReligionArt. 25 – 28
Cultural & EducationalArt. 29 – 30
Constitutional RemediesArt. 32
Breakdown of Fundamental Right Categories

1. Right to Equality (Articles 14–18): Article 14 (Equality before Law), Article 15 (Prohibition of Discrimination), Article 16 (Equality of Opportunity in Public Employment), Article 17 (Abolition of Untouchability), Article 18 (Abolition of Titles).

2. Right to Freedom (Articles 19–22): Article 19 (6 Freedoms: Speech, Assembly, Association, Movement, Residence, Profession), Article 20 (Protection in respect of conviction), Article 21 (Protection of Life & Personal Liberty), Article 21A (Right to Education), Article 22 (Protection against arrest and detention).

3. Right Against Exploitation (Articles 23–24): Article 23 (Prohibition of human trafficking & forced labor), Article 24 (Prohibition of child labor in hazardous employment).

4. Freedom of Religion (Articles 25–28): Article 25 (Conscience & free profession), Article 26 (Manage religious affairs), Article 27 (Tax exemption for promoting religion), Article 28 (Freedom from religious instruction in state institutions).

5. Cultural & Educational Rights (Articles 29–30): Article 29 (Protection of interests of minorities), Article 30 (Right of minorities to establish educational institutions).

The 5 Constitutional Writs (Article 32 & 226)
  • Habeas Corpus ("To have the body of"): Protects against illegal detention; orders the detaining authority to produce the person.
  • Mandamus ("We Command"): Directs a public official or body to perform a mandatory statutory duty.
  • Prohibition: Issued by higher court to stop a lower court or tribunal from exceeding its jurisdiction.
  • Certiorari ("To be certified"): Quashes an illegal order already passed by a lower court or quasi-judicial body.
  • Quo-Warranto ("By what authority?"): Inquires into the legality of a person holding a public office.

6. DPSP & Fundamental Duties

Directive Principles of State Policy (Part IV, Art. 36–51): Borrowed from Ireland; social and economic guidelines for establishing a welfare state. Non-justiciable in court.

  • Socialistic Principles: Article 38 (Promote welfare), Article 39 (Equal pay for equal work), Article 41 (Right to work), Article 43 (Living wage).
  • Gandhian Principles: Article 40 (Organize Village Panchayats), Article 47 (Prohibit intoxicating drinks), Article 48 (Prohibit cow slaughter).
  • Liberal-Intellectual Principles: Article 44 (Uniform Civil Code - UCC), Article 45 (Early childhood care), Article 50 (Separation of Judiciary from Executive), Article 51 (International peace).
Fundamental Duties (Part IV-A, Article 51A)

Added by the 42nd Amendment (1976) on the recommendation of the Swaran Singh Committee. Originally 10 duties; the 11th duty was added by the 86th Amendment (2002).

Key Duties: Abide by Constitution & respect Flag/Anthem, cherish ideals of freedom struggle, protect sovereignty & integrity of India, defend nation, promote harmony, preserve rich heritage, protect environment, develop scientific temper, safeguard public property, strive for excellence, and provide education to children aged 6–14.

7. Citizenship (Articles 5–11)

Part II of the Constitution deals with citizenship at the commencement of the Constitution on January 26, 1950. Single Citizenship is provided across India.

  • Article 5: Citizenship by domicile in India.
  • Article 6 & 7: Citizenship rights of migrants from and to Pakistan.
  • Article 8: Citizenship of persons of Indian origin residing outside India.
  • Article 9: Person voluntarily acquiring foreign citizenship loses Indian citizenship.
  • Article 11: Parliament has sole power to regulate citizenship by law (Citizenship Act, 1955).

8. Union Executive

Part V (Articles 52–78) covers the Union Executive consisting of the President, Vice-President, Prime Minister, Council of Ministers, and Attorney General.

President of India (Articles 52–62)

Constitutional Head of State and supreme commander of defense forces.

  • Election (Art. 54 & 55): Elected by an Electoral College consisting of elected members of Lok Sabha, Rajya Sabha, and State Legislative Assemblies (including Delhi and Puducherry) using proportional representation by single transferable vote.
  • Powers: Executive, Legislative (Ordinances - Art. 123), Judicial (Pardoning power - Art. 72), Emergency powers (Art. 352, 356, 360).
  • Impeachment (Art. 61): Can be impeached for "violation of the Constitution."
Prime Minister & Council of Ministers (Articles 74–75)

Prime Minister: Real executive authority (de facto head of government). Appointed by President.

Council of Ministers: Article 75 states that the Council of Ministers is collectively responsible to the Lok Sabha. Total ministers cannot exceed 15% of Lok Sabha strength (91st Amendment).

9. Parliament of India & Law-Making

The Union Parliament consists of the President and two Houses: Lok Sabha (House of the People) and Rajya Sabha (Council of States).

Feature Lok Sabha (Lower House) Rajya Sabha (Upper House)
NatureTemporary (Dissolved every 5 years)Permanent (1/3rd members retire every 2 years)
Max Strength550 members250 members (12 nominated by President)
Presiding OfficerSpeaker (Elected by members)Vice-President (Ex-officio Chairman)
Financial PowerExclusive power over Money BillsCan only delay Money Bill for 14 days
Types of Bills & Law-Making Process
  • Ordinary Bill: Can be introduced in either House; resolved by Joint Sitting (Art. 108) if deadlock occurs.
  • Money Bill (Art. 110): Introduced only in Lok Sabha on President's recommendation; certified by Speaker.
  • Financial Bill: Contains financial provisions alongside general legislation.
  • Constitutional Amendment Bill (Art. 368): Requires special majority; no provision for joint sitting.

10. State Government

Part VI (Articles 152–237) prescribes the state administrative structure.

  • Governor (Art. 153–161): Executive head of the State appointed by President. Holds office during President's pleasure. Has pardoning power (Art. 161) and ordinance-making power (Art. 213).
  • Chief Minister & Cabinet: Real executive head at state level; responsible to State Legislative Assembly.
  • State Legislature: Bicameral in 6 states (Andhra Pradesh, Telangana, Uttar Pradesh, Bihar, Maharashtra, Karnataka) with Legislative Assembly (Vidhan Sabha) and Legislative Council (Vidhan Parishad).

11. The Judiciary

India features an integrated, single judicial hierarchy: Supreme Court → High Courts → Subordinate Courts.

Supreme Court of India (Articles 124–147)

Highest judicial court and final court of appeal. Guardian of the Constitution.

  • Original Jurisdiction (Art. 131): Inter-governmental disputes (Centre vs States, State vs State).
  • Writ Jurisdiction (Art. 32): Direct enforcement of Fundamental Rights.
  • Appellate Jurisdiction (Art. 132–134): Appeals in civil, criminal, and constitutional matters.
  • Advisory Jurisdiction (Art. 143): President can seek SC's opinion on questions of law/fact.
  • Court of Record (Art. 129): Has power to punish for contempt of itself.
High Courts & Subordinate Courts (Articles 214–237)

High Courts are apex courts at state level. Article 226 gives High Courts wider writ jurisdiction than Article 32 (can issue writs for Fundamental Rights and ordinary legal rights).

12. Centre-State Relations & 7th Schedule

Part XI and XII divide legislative, administrative, and financial powers between Union and States.

The 3 Legislative Lists (Seventh Schedule)
List Name Subjects Count Key Examples
Union List100 subjectsDefense, Foreign Affairs, Banking, Railways, Atomic Energy, Currency.
State List61 subjectsPublic Order, Police, Public Health, Agriculture, Local Govt, Prisons.
Concurrent List52 subjectsEducation, Forests, Criminal Law, Marriage, Trade Unions, Electricity.

Residuary Powers (Art. 248): Power to legislate on subjects not mentioned in any list rests exclusively with the Union Parliament.

13. Local Self-Government (Panchayats & Municipalities)

Constitutionalized by landmark amendments in 1992 to decentralize democracy to grassroot level.

  • 73rd Amendment Act (1992): Added Part IX and 11th Schedule (29 functional items) for Panchayati Raj. 3-tier system: Gram Panchayat (village), Panchayat Samiti (block), Zilla Parishad (district).
  • 74th Amendment Act (1992): Added Part IX-A and 12th Schedule (18 functional items) for Urban Local Bodies (Nagar Panchayats, Municipal Councils, Municipal Corporations).

15. Emergency Provisions (Articles 352–360)

Part XVIII enables the Centre to meet extraordinary situations and safeguard sovereignty.

Type Article Grounds
National EmergencyArt. 352War, External Aggression, or Armed Rebellion (changed from internal disturbance by 44th Amendment).
President's RuleArt. 356Failure of constitutional machinery in a State.
Financial EmergencyArt. 360Threat to financial stability or credit of India (Never invoked so far).

16. Constitutional Amendments (Article 368)

Article 368 in Part XX specifies procedure to amend the Constitution by Simple Majority, Special Majority, or Special Majority with Ratification by half of State Legislatures.

Landmark Amendments Reference
  • 1st Amendment (1951): Added 9th Schedule to protect land reform laws from judicial review.
  • 42nd Amendment (1976) - "Mini-Constitution": Added Socialist, Secular, Integrity to Preamble; added Fundamental Duties (Part IV-A); altered federal balance.
  • 44th Amendment (1978): Undid 42nd Amendment excesses; replaced 'internal disturbance' with 'armed rebellion'; made Right to Property a legal right (Art. 300A).
  • 52nd Amendment (1985): Added 10th Schedule (Anti-Defection Law).
  • 61st Amendment (1989): Reduced voting age from 21 to 18 years (Art. 326).
  • 73rd & 74th Amendments (1992): Constitutionalized Panchayats and Municipalities.
  • 86th Amendment (2002): Right to Education made Fundamental Right (Art. 21A).
  • 101st Amendment (2016): Introduced Goods and Services Tax (GST).
  • 103rd Amendment (2019): 10% EWS Reservation in jobs and education.
  • 106th Amendment (2023): Nari Shakti Vandan Adhiniyam - 33% reservation for women in Lok Sabha and State Assemblies.

18. Landmark Supreme Court Judgments

  • Kesavananda Bharati v. State of Kerala (1973): Historic 13-judge bench ruled that Parliament can amend any part of the Constitution under Art. 368 but cannot alter its "Basic Structure".
  • Maneka Gandhi v. Union of India (1978): Expanded Article 21; procedure established by law must be 'just, fair, and reasonable'.
  • Minerva Mills v. Union of India (1980): Reaffirmed that judicial review and harmony between Fundamental Rights and DPSPs are basic features.
  • S. R. Bommai v. Union of India (1994): Restricted arbitrary misuse of Article 356 (President's Rule); held secularism as a basic feature.
  • Justice K. S. Puttaswamy v. Union of India (2017): Unanimously declared Right to Privacy as an intrinsic part of Right to Life under Article 21.

19. Elections & Electoral Process

Universal Adult Suffrage (Article 326): Every citizen of India who is not less than 18 years of age has the right to vote without discrimination.

Anti-Defection Law (Tenth Schedule): Disqualifies legislators who voluntarily give up party membership or vote against party whip during parliamentary divisions.

20. General Constitution Knowledge

Who drafted the Constitution?

The Constituent Assembly of India framed it, while the 7-member Drafting Committee chaired by Dr. B. R. Ambedkar drafted the legal text.

Why is January 26 chosen as Republic Day?

To commemorate the Poorna Swaraj (Complete Independence) declaration passed by the Indian National Congress at its Lahore Session on Jan 26, 1930.

What happens if a law violates the Constitution?

Under Article 13, the judiciary (Supreme Court / High Court) can declare any law inconsistent with or in derogation of Fundamental Rights as void and unconstitutional.

FREQUENTLY ASKED QUESTIONS

What is the Preamble of the Constitution?
The Preamble is the introductory statement outlining guiding objectives (Justice, Liberty, Equality, Fraternity).
How many Fundamental Rights are there?
There are 6 Fundamental Rights in Part III (Articles 12-35).
How can the Constitution be amended?
Under Article 368 by simple majority, special majority, or ratification by half of states.
What is the Basic Structure Doctrine?
Established in Kesavananda Bharati case (1973), it rules that Parliament cannot alter basic principles like secularism, federalism, and judicial review.
